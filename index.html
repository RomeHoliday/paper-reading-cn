Training jobs on one feeder peak together: each GPU draws about 700 W while computing and about 140 W while communicating.

Where the problem sits

AI training

A training step is compute kernels, then a collective. Power is high, then low. For an H100-class machine that shape is drawn here as about 700 W in compute and about 140 W in communication. Fifty thousand GPUs in one synchronous job would swing between about 35 MW and 7 MW. Those watts are an overlay for the lecture, not a meter on the laptop that drew this page.

The GPU

Delaying one synchronous job only slides its wave. The height of that one job does not fall. The decision appears when several independent jobs share a feeder and each has slack before its deadline.

The datacenter

The feeder is sized for the coincident peak. On eight identical jobs, computing together draws 8×700 = 5600 W. The next two seconds the same feeder carries 8×140 = 1120 W. Spare power in the valley cannot take another job if the breaker was already sized to the peak. The compute phase of a new job still lands on that peak.

AI training produces the square wave, the GPU is where the watts are, and the datacenter pays for the moment those waves line up.

Why the peak matters

Moving the same intervals does not change the useful energy. Eight identical jobs use 8×(700×16 + 140×16)/3600 = 29.87 Wh whether they are aligned or staggered. An offset changes the peak and the shape of the sum.

  • Filling the valley with dummy work leaves the peak at 5600 W and adds 17.42 Wh that does not train.
  • Cutting compute power by 25% takes the peak from 5600 W to 4200 W and stretches the finish from 32 s to 37.3 s. The step itself gets slower.
  • The price leaves both watt levels alone. The same eight jobs go from 5600 W to 3360 W, finish at 34 s, deadline 40 s, and miss nobody. 3360 W is 4×700 + 4×140.

What is being bought is room on the feeder, not a lower GPU power limit and not fewer watt-hours of training.

Related work

Zeus

Zeus (You, Chung, and Chowdhury, NSDI 2023) chooses a batch size and a GPU power limit, and reports energy-to-accuracy against time-to-accuracy. It profiles power limits in the first epoch and samples batch sizes. This lecture leaves the watt levels fixed and moves the start of a square wave that already has its shape, subject to a deadline.

Power Stabilization

Power Stabilization (Choukse et al., arXiv:2508.14318, 2025) treats the square wave as a plant problem and fills the communication valley with dummy GEMM or a firmware floor. On the eight-job trace here, that fill costs 17.42 Wh and leaves the peak at 5600 W.

POLCA

POLCA (Patel et al., ASPLOS 2024) measures power swings of cloud LLM training and the effect of frequency caps on A100s. A cap changes how high compute is and how long it lasts. A 25% compute-power cut in this simulator lands at 4200 W and 37.3 s.

Deadline scheduling

Deadline-aware GPU schedulers choose which job runs under a time constraint. They do not model a compute/communication square wave.

A price for a shared constraint

Optimization flow control (Low and Lapsley, IEEE/ACM Trans. Networking, 1999) turns a capacity constraint into a price, and each source solves its own problem. The Adaptive Charging Network (Lee, Low, et al., IEEE Trans. Smart Grid, 2021) uses that idea for EV charging under a deadline. Those results are for divisible rates or currents, and for convex programs. This lecture uses the price on a discrete start offset. It does not inherit the convergence theorem. Greening geographical load balancing (Liu, Lin, Wierman, Low, and Andrew, SIGMETRICS 2011) prices electricity across data centers, not phases inside one room.

The decision here is the offset of a square wave inside one room. The numbers are peak, finish time, deadline misses, and dummy energy, from a laptop simulator.

How a job picks its start

A job can only slide the moment it begins. A price marks which moments are already busy. The job keeps the start with the smallest bill.

1. One GPU

Every 4 seconds the GPU repeats two levels: 2 s of compute at 700 W, then 2 s of communication at 140 W. Eight repeats. Eight such GPUs share one feeder.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{aligned} &= 8\times 700 = 5600\,\mathrm{W} \\ \text{staggered} &= 4\times 700 + 4\times 140 = 3360\,\mathrm{W} \end{aligned} \]

2. The only choice

A job may wait before it starts. That wait is φ, in seconds. The deadline allows up to 4 s, tried every 0.1 s.

\[ \varphi \in \{0,\, 0.1,\, 0.2,\, \ldots,\, 4.0\} \]

3. The price of each moment

The room looks at every GPU except the one deciding. Busy moments get almost all of the price. The prices add up to 1.

\[ z(t)=\frac{P_{-i}(t)-\max_u P_{-i}(u)}{280} \]
\[ \lambda(t)=\dfrac{e^{z(t)}}{\displaystyle\sum_u e^{z(u)}} \]
t
One moment on the clock. Here each moment is 0.1 seconds long.
i
The job that is choosing right now. For the first choice, i is job 0.
P
Power, in watts. A bigger P means the GPUs are drawing more electricity at that moment.
P−i(t)
The watts of every GPU except job i, at moment t. Job i’s own watts are left out, so it is not charged for crowding it created itself.
u
Just another moment, used while we look across the whole clock.
maxu
The busiest moment: the largest watt reading among all the others.
280
The temperature, in watts. The formula divides by it. It is written τ = 280. A small temperature makes the price sit almost only on the busiest moment.
z(t)
The temperature score. z(t) = (watts at this moment − watts at the busiest moment) / 280. The busiest moment is 0. Every quieter moment is negative.
ez
Turns that score into a weight. A score of 0 has weight 1. A score of −14 has a weight so small it is practically 0.
Σu
Add the weights of every moment together.
λ(t)
The price of moment t. It is that moment’s weight divided by the total of all weights. Every price is at least 0, and all of them add up to 1.

Job 0’s first look, while the other seven are still together:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{compute:}\quad 7\times 700 &= 4900\,\mathrm{W} \\ z &= \frac{4900-4900}{280}=0 \\ e^{z} &= 1 \\[0.6em] \text{communication:}\quad 7\times 140 &= 980\,\mathrm{W} \\ z &= \frac{980-4900}{280}=-14 \\ e^{z} &\approx 0 \end{aligned} \]

160 busy slices share the price. Each 2 s compute block gets 12.5%. Each communication block gets 0.

4. The bill

The job tries each φ and keeps the smallest cost. Moving has a small penalty, so a tie stays put.

\[ \mathrm{cost}(\varphi)=\sum_t \lambda(t)\,P_i(t;\varphi)+\rho\,\lvert\varphi-\varphi_{\mathrm{now}}\rvert \]
φ
How many seconds this job waits before it starts. The choices are 0, 0.1, 0.2, …, 4.0.
φnow
The wait it already has. At the start, that is 0.
Pi(t; φ)
Job i’s own watts at moment t, if it waits φ seconds. Compute is 700 W. Communication is 140 W.
λ(t)
The price of moment t, from the formula above.
Σt
Add up, over every moment, price times this job’s own watts. That sum is the bill.
|φ − φnow|
How many seconds the start would move. The straight bars mean “ignore the minus sign.”
ρ
The charge for each second the start moves.
cost(φ)
The bill, plus the charge for moving. The job keeps the wait with the smallest cost.

Job 0, first turn:

\[ \begin{aligned} \varphi&=0 &&\rightarrow& 700 \\ \varphi&=2 &&\rightarrow& 138 &&\text{chosen} \\ \varphi&=4 &&\rightarrow& 644 \end{aligned} \]

At φ = 2 its 700 W sits on slices whose price is 0, so the bill collapses. The charge for waiting those 2 seconds is small beside that drop.

The dual

The price λ(t) above is the dual variable. The feeder limit is not handed to the job. It is carried by λ. The line the job solves is the bill just defined:

\[ \varphi_i \leftarrow \arg\min_{\varphi}\; \sum_t \lambda(t)\, P_i(t;\varphi) + \rho\,\lvert\varphi-\varphi_{\mathrm{now}}\rvert \]

One job answers, the load changes, λ is posted again, and the next job answers. The peak is measured after those choices.

5. Then the next job

One job decides, the room rebuilds λ, the next job is asked. Jobs 0, 1, 2, and 3 each take +2 s. The feeder steps 5600 → 5040 → 4480 → 3920 → 3360.

Job 4 then sees two even halves:

\[ \begin{aligned} \varphi &= 0 &&\rightarrow& 200 &&\text{chosen} \\ \varphi &= 2 &&\rightarrow& 655 \\ \varphi &= 4 &&\rightarrow& 214 \end{aligned} \]

Sliding 4 s would shave the bill a little, then the move penalty puts it back above 200. Job 4 stays. Jobs 5, 6, and 7 do the same.

A second pass asks everyone again. No cost falls. The loop stops at φ = [2, 2, 2, 2, 0, 0, 0, 0], peak 3360 W, finish 34 s, no deadline miss.

Three harder pictures

One still frame of the drifting-period case. In the HTML page these three cases can be switched and stepped through. The PDF cannot.

Drifting periods

Eight jobs, periods 4.0, 4.0, 4.0, 4.0, 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, and 4.4 s, for 120 s. Aligned peak 5600 W. Delaying half the jobs by 2 s is also 5600 W, because the phases slip back together. The price holds 3920 W. No deadline miss. This is not a proof that the price found the lowest possible peak.

Unequal periods

Periods 3, 4, 5, 6, 3, 5, 5, and 4 s. Aligned 5600 W, a fixed 2 s delay 5040 W, the price 3920 W. No miss.

Different GPU counts

Jobs with 1, 1, 2, 2, 4, 4, 8, and 8 GPUs (each still 2 s at 700 W and 2 s at 140 W). Here the jobs arrive one at a time, and each answers the price of the jobs already running. Aligned 21000 W, half delay 17640 W, price 12600 W. No choice of starts does better: on average 15 of the 30 GPUs are computing, so some moment has at least 15 computing, and 15×700 + 15×140 = 12600 W.

If all eight start aligned and then answer in turn, as in the first picture, the same price stops at 13160 W = 16×700 + 14×140. Both 8-GPU jobs end up in the same half, and moving any one job alone does not lower the peak. These cases are why a price beats always delaying half the jobs by 2 s.
